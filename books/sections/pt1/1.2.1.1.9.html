Significant Figures

  1. The value of any digit in a number is dependent upon its position. We call the first non-zero digit the first significant figure and the last non-zero digit the last significant figure. These three numbers all have 4 significant figures.

  1. Further examples:

a. 14384 (5 sig fig) e. 1.005 (4 sig fig)

b. 0.089 (2 sig fig) f. 72300 (3 sig fig)

c. 29.08604 (7 sig fig) g. 10000000 (1 sig fig)

d. 72 (2 sig fig) h. 630100 (4 sig fig)

  1. To write a number to a given number of significant figures we look at one more significant figure than is required and if this digit is 5 or more, we round up the preceding figure; 4 or less we leave the preceding figure as it is.

For example:

    1. 187340 is 187000 to 3 significant figures 190000 to 2 significant figures.
    2. 0.001239 is 0.00124 to 3 significant figures, 0.0012 to 2 significant figures.
    3. 35.603 is 35.6 to 3 significant figures, 36 to 2 significant figures.
    4. 0.081778 is 0.08178 to 4 significant figures, 0.0818 to 3 significant figures.
    5. 1.006 is 1 to one significant figure.

Decimal Places

  1. When correcting to a certain number of decimal places (rounding off) it is only the digit immediately following the required decimal place which affects it. If the digit immediately following is 5 or more, the previous digit should be increased by 1. If it is less than 5 the previous digit is unaltered. For example:
  2. 0.833 = 0.83 to 2 decimal places.
  3. 0.866 = 0.87 to 2 decimal places.
  4. 0.2346 = 0.23 to 2 decimal places.
  5. 127.12304 = 127.1 to one decimal place
  6. 0.0001449 = 0.00014 to 5 decimal places

1.2.1.1.10 Define and use types of decimal numbers

  1. Decimals are an extension of the place values in the denary system and are used to express values less than one. In this respect they may be regarded as being the same as fractions.

Types of Decimal Numbers

  1. Decimal numbers can be categorised as terminating and non-terminating.

Terminating decimals

  1. These decimal numbers have a finite number of digits after the decimal point. These types of decimal numbers are also known as exact decimal numbers. The number of digits after the decimal point of these numbers is countable.

For example: 57.24, -3.5, 7.62, etc.

  1. These are terminating decimals, because the number of digits after the decimal point is finite.

Non-terminating decimals

  1. Non-terminating decimals have an infinite number of digits after the decimal point, and they continue endlessly. These decimal numbers can be further categorised as follows:

a. Non-recurring decimal numbers, which have an infinite number of digits after the decimal point, but these digits do not follow a specific order.

For example

152.93687493042058...etc., etc.,

b. Recurring Decimal Numbers, which have an infinite number of digits after the decimal point, but, the digits are repeated at regular intervals.

  1. All repeating decimal numbers are rational, that is, they can be represented in the form where a and b are whole numbers.

For example, the decimal representation of is 0.333…

(spoken as “0.3 repeating” or “0.3 recurring”).

This becomes periodic just after the decimal point, repeating the single digit sequence 3 infinitely.

A more complicated example is which, with long division becomes: 5.8144144… where the decimal representation becomes periodic after the first digit, repeating the sequence 144 infinitely.

Notation

  1. There are several accepted methods of showing repeating or recurring decimals. One method is to put a horizontal line (known as a vinculum) above the repeating digits, or place dots above the outermost numerals of the repeating digits. An alternative method is to enclose the repeating digits in brackets. Another convention is to repeat the sequence at least twice and then show further repetitions with an ellipsis (…), this method has become almost universal in printed documents, because it does not require the use of special characters. 1.2.1.1.11 Convert between fractions, decimals and PERCENTAGES

  1. The Imperial system of measurement makes extensive use of fractions to express partial quantities. However, there are occasions where these partial quantities are expressed as decimals. For instance, the diameter of a fastener hole may be given as a fraction of an inch and the clearance to allow the fastener to pass through the hole given as thousandths of an inch expressed as a decimal to three places.
  2. Choosing the correct size of drill bit to cut a clearance hole for a fastener is an example of an occasion where it will be necessary to add a fraction to a decimal. This will involve either converting a fraction into a decimal or converting a decimal into a fraction.

Converting repeating decimals to fractions

  1. Repeating decimals are difficult to work with (you cannot, for instance, enter them into a calculator) and their use inevitably results in an approximation. For this reason, if possible, they are best avoided. As stated previously, all repeating decimals numbers are rational, which means that they can be expressed in the form where a and b are whole numbers.   1. Any repeating decimal can be dealt with in this manner, some will cancel down into small fractions, and some will not. However, it is quite feasible to enter even large fractions into a calculator, thus avoiding the need for an approximation.

  1. Calculating the percentage increase, or decrease is a method to show how much something has changed between measurements.

Method

    1. work out the difference between the two numbers being compared
    2. divide the difference by the original number
